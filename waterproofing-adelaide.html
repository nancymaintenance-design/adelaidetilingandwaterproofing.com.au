Waterproofing Adelaide

Waterproofing in Adelaide for wet and exposed areas

Waterproofing in Adelaide spans more than bathrooms. Ellis Services Group handles enquiries for laundries, balconies, roofs, kitchens, pool-related areas and external surfaces. Share the surface, its current condition and intended finish so we can respond to the work requested.

Waterproofing in a home is not one job — it is a set of different jobs, one for each wet area, and the right fix depends on which one has failed. A shower recess that leaks at the hob is a different problem from a balcony that drips onto the unit below, and both are different from a laundry floor that has been quietly wet for months.

This page runs through which areas need waterproofing, the signs a membrane has failed, how to tell a localised repair from a job that needs redoing, what happens after the membrane goes down, and which standard applies.

Apartments and units are a different proposition from a freestanding house — balconies over habitable space, shared drainage, body corporate approvals and access for strip-out all change the scope, so the wet area is only half the question. If your job is a bathroom renovation or a leaking shower, there is a dedicated page for bathroom waterproofing in Adelaide.

Bathroom and shower areas

Bathrooms are the most common waterproofing job in Adelaide, and the one most often bundled into a renovation. The membrane has to work with the floor waste, the hob, and every penetration for taps and spouts. Because the detail sits under the finish, the documentation matters as much as the work.

Shower recesses

A shower recess concentrates water in a small area, day after day. The usual failure points are the hob or threshold, the internal corners, and the waste itself. Leaks here often present as damage outside the shower rather than inside it.

Balconies are the wet area most often left off waterproofing pages, despite being a genuine and recurring problem in Adelaide's apartment stock. Drainage and movement joints are as important as the membrane.

Signs a waterproofing membrane has failed

These are all things you can check from floor level:

  • Grout that keeps crumbling or powdering after repeated re-grouting.
  • Silicone that repeatedly lifts or peels away at junctions.
  • Efflorescence — white, powdery deposits along grout lines.
  • A musty or damp smell that does not clear with ventilation.
  • Staining, bubbling paint or swollen plaster on a ceiling or wall below.
  • Skirting boards or adjacent cabinetry that have swollen or discoloured.
  • Tiles that sound hollow over a widening area, or that start to lift.

Do not remove tiles, cut into walls or climb onto a roof to investigate — that is work for someone with the right equipment and access.

Repair or re-do? How to tell

Localised joint or silicone failure

If the problem is a single joint, a section of silicone, or an isolated crack, a targeted repair is usually enough. This is the lowest-cost outcome and the most common one when a leak is caught early.

Recurrent leaks in the same spot

A leak that returns after the joint has been redone is not a joint problem. It usually means water is getting past the membrane somewhere else and reappearing at the same weak point. At that stage the membrane needs assessment.

Signs the substrate is affected

Swollen plaster, soft or springy flooring, tiles lifting across an area, or staining that keeps spreading point to water having reached the structure. Once the substrate is affected, the finish has to come off to deal with it properly.

What happens after the membrane

Flood testing before anything goes on top

Once the membrane has cured, the wet area is flood tested: the waste is plugged, the area is filled to a level just below the hob or threshold, the level is marked, and it is checked again after standing. A drop means water is escaping. The test happens before anything is laid over the membrane — never after.

Handing over to tiling

With the test passed, the job hands over to tiling. The membrane is a working surface at that point, not a finished one: adhesives, trowels and movement joints all have to suit the system underneath. Because Ellis handles both scopes, that handover is coordinated rather than negotiated between two trades — see tiling after waterproofing.

Standards and documentation

Wet-area waterproofing in Australia is carried out with reference to AS 3740:2021 (Waterproofing of domestic wet areas, Standards Australia, committee BD-038), which replaced AS 3740:2010 and applies to Class 1 and Class 2 buildings. It sets out requirements for materials, design and installation, including how far a membrane must extend up walls and how junctions are treated.

Separately, in South Australia, building work contracting is regulated: the Consumer and Business Services (CBS) building work contractor's licence regime applies to contractors carrying out building work in the state. Ask which standard your scope is being carried out to, ask what documentation comes with the work, and ask about licensing and insurance when you enquire.

Waterproofing FAQs

Is waterproofing required before tiling a wet area?

Yes. Tiles and grout are not a waterproof layer — water passes through grout. The membrane beneath the tile bed does the work.

How long does a membrane last?

It depends on the system, the substrate and how the area is used. A membrane is not intended to last as long as the house, and wet areas that have never been opened up in decades should be assessed before re-tiling.

Can waterproofing be done without removing tiles?

Sometimes, where the failure is localised and the substrate is sound. If the substrate is affected, the tiles need to come up.

What should I ask for when the job is finished?

Ask what standard the work was carried out to, what the flood test result was, and what documentation comes with the job. Keep it — it matters for insurance and for future work.
