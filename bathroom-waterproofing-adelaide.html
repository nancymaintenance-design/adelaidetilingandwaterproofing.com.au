Bathroom waterproofing Adelaide

Bathroom waterproofing in Adelaide for showers and renovations

Most Adelaide bathrooms don't fail because the tiles were bad. They fail because of a layer you never see — the membrane underneath them. By the time water shows up on the ceiling below, or the grout starts crumbling, the tile work is usually the least of the problem.

This page is for Adelaide homeowners who are about to renovate a bathroom, or who have started noticing the early signs of a leak. Bathroom waterproofing Adelaide jobs usually fall into one of two situations: planned work, where the room is being stripped back anyway, or reactive work, where a leak has already started showing somewhere it shouldn't.

The sequence is the same in both cases — what changes is how much has to come off first. This page covers what AS 3740:2021 requires in a bathroom wet area, the order waterproofing and tiling need to happen in, how a flood test works, what the work typically involves, and what to have ready before you ask for a quote.

When a bathroom needs waterproofing

Renovation and re-tiling

If you are stripping a bathroom back to the substrate, the old membrane goes out with the old tiles. New waterproofing is not optional at that point — it is part of the rebuild. It is also the point at which the work costs least to do properly, because everything is already open and accessible. If you are at the planning stage, see preparing a bathroom renovation for waterproofing.

Signs the existing membrane has failed

Watch for grout that keeps crumbling no matter how often it is redone, silicone that lifts away from the junction between floor and wall, a musty smell that lingers near the shower, or staining appearing on the ceiling of the room below. Efflorescence — a white, powdery deposit along grout lines — is another common signal, because it means water is travelling through the tile bed and carrying salts to the surface.

Bathrooms that have never been re-waterproofed

A membrane does not last indefinitely. If your bathroom has not been opened up in twenty years and you are about to re-tile, assume the membrane needs attention rather than hoping it survives the work.

What AS 3740:2021 covers in a bathroom

AS 3740:2021, Waterproofing of domestic wet areas, is the Australian Standard published by Standards Australia and prepared by committee BD-038. It replaced AS 3740:2010 and applies to wet areas in Class 1 and Class 2 buildings — in plain terms, houses and low-rise residential units. The standard sets out requirements for the materials, design and installation of wet-area waterproofing, including how far the membrane must extend up walls, how junctions and penetrations are treated, and how the work is to be documented.

Wet areas inside the bathroom

Within a bathroom, the shower recess carries the highest risk, followed by the floor generally, the hob or threshold at the shower entry, and the wall junctions. Bath and spa surrounds, plus the areas behind vanities and around tap penetrations, also fall within scope.

Why the membrane is separate from the tiles

Tiles and grout are not a waterproof layer. Water passes through grout as a matter of course. The membrane sits beneath the tile bed and does the actual work of keeping water out of the structure. That is why re-grouting or re-siliconing treats the symptom rather than the cause. Ask which standard your scope is being carried out to, and ask what documentation comes with the work.

Waterproofing before tiling — the order that matters

This is the part renovation quotes most often get wrong. Waterproofing and tiling are two different scopes, and if they are booked in the wrong order — or by two parties who never speak to each other — you can end up paying for tile work that has to come back off.

The sequence on a typical Adelaide bathroom job

  1. Strip-out of existing tiles, bed and fittings.
  2. Substrate preparation and screed to falls, so water moves toward the waste.
  3. Primer applied to the prepared substrate.
  4. Bond breaker and flashing tape at all junctions, corners and penetrations.
  5. Membrane applied in coats, with reinforcement at the junctions.
  6. Cure time, as specified by the membrane manufacturer.
  7. Flood test of the shower recess and floor.
  8. Tiling and grouting over the cured, tested membrane.
  9. Silicone at flexible joints — not grout, which cracks at any change of plane.

Who to book first

The waterproofer goes in first and the tiler follows. The handover point is step seven: nothing gets tiled until the flood test has been done and passed. If you are engaging two separate trades, make sure both know where that handover sits, and make sure the tiler is not scheduled before the membrane has cured. When both scopes sit with one provider, that coordination is handled for you — see tiling after waterproofing.

How a flood test works

A flood test confirms the membrane actually holds water before anything is laid over it. The waste is plugged, the recess or floor is filled to a set level just below the hob or threshold, and that level is marked. The water is left to stand — typically overnight, and at minimum for several hours — and the level is checked again. A drop means water is escaping somewhere. The test is done before tiling and never after, because a membrane failure discovered under new tiles means the tiles come off.

What bathroom waterproofing costs in Adelaide

Cost moves with the size of the wet area, how much strip-out is involved, whether the substrate needs repair or re-screeding, the membrane system specified, and whether this is a standalone repair or part of a full renovation. As a broad indication, a shower recess sits in a different band from a full bathroom, and a job that also needs substrate work sits higher again. Treat any figure you find online as indicative only — it is not a quote. Ask for a written scope that separates strip-out, substrate work, membrane, flood testing and documentation, so you can compare like with like.

What to have ready before you ask for a quote

Having these six things ready will get you a more accurate figure, and usually a faster one:

  1. Rough room or shower dimensions.
  2. Which floor the bathroom is on, and whether there is a room beneath it.
  3. Access details — stairs, lift, parking, any site restrictions.
  4. Whether tiles have already been selected, or whether that is still open.
  5. Whether there is an active leak now, and where it is showing.
  6. Your timing — planned renovation, or something that needs attention soon.

Bathroom waterproofing FAQs

Can you waterproof over existing tiles?

Sometimes, where the tiles are sound and the problem is localised. It depends entirely on what is underneath. If the substrate is affected, the tiles need to come up.

How long does the work take?

Strip-out, substrate work and membrane application run over several days, and the membrane needs to cure before the flood test. Tiling follows once the test passes.

Can I use the bathroom during the work?

No. It is out of action from strip-out through to the silicone setting.

Do I need to replace the whole bathroom?

Not always. A localised joint or silicone failure can be repaired. Leaks that keep returning to the same spot usually point to the membrane.

What if the leak is already showing downstairs?

That means water has been moving for some time. Stop using the shower and have the membrane assessed before anything is re-tiled.
